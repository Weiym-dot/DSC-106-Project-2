Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

May Wang, Yimeng Wei, Zach Ong — maw044@ucsd.edu, yiw218@ucsd.edu, z2ong@ucsd.edu

Proposition: The gender gap in secondary-school enrollment has largely closed worldwide.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Grouping gap sizes — Score: +1. We grouped countries into three gap ranges to make the pattern easier to see. The small-gap share rose from 59% to 69%, supporting the proposition. However, the five-point cutoff can hide differences within that group. So, showing every country would give more detail but make the chart harder to read.
  • Using bars and color — Score: +1.5. We used two equal-length stacked bars to compare the gap categories in 2000 and 2018. Dark teal highlights small gaps and draws attention to progress, while lighter colors describe larger gaps. Separate bars can show each category’s change more clearly but weaken the overall message.
  • Highlighting progress with text — Score: +1. The title highlights that more countries have small enrollment gaps. The label shows that this number grew from 57 to 66 countries. The drop in the median gap from 4.0 to 2.6 percentage points adds more support. They all convey the message of greater equality. A neutral title would be less persuasive. However, the chart covers only 96 countries through 2018, so it cannot fully support a claim about the whole world today.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Data choosing and cutoff — Score: -1.5 The measurement of the worldwide gender gap in secondary-school enrollment is blurry. The overall mean gap across all countries may be small, so we chose to show individual-country data instead. In fact, most countries have gender gaps within 0–10 percentage points. However, because we wanted readers to notice that there are still many countries with relatively large gaps, we set the graph cutoff at 5 rather than 0 and removed countries with gaps below 5. This makes countries with gaps above 10 appear to occupy a larger share of the visualization than they would if all countries were shown.
  • Different color point and dispersion degree — Score: -2 Red and darker colors usually attract more visual attention, so the points become progressively redder and darker as the gender gap increases. In addition, because the y-coordinate does not encode any actual variable, we adjusted the vertical dispersion so that points between 10 and 25 percentage points are more concentrated near the center of the graph. This makes the larger-gap countries easier to notice and visually groups them together, which can make them appear more prevalent than they actually are.
  • Reference line and background hint — Score: -1.5 We added reference lines at 10 and 15 percentage points and divided the background into increasingly stronger color regions: pale yellow for 5–10, light orange for 10–15, and light red for 15–25. These visual thresholds encourage readers to interpret gaps above 10, and especially above 15, as increasingly serious even though these cutoff values are design choices rather than universally defined standards. The background shading also creates a visual progression from less concerning to more concerning gaps, which draws more attention toward the right side of the chart.
  • Title — Score: -1 The title, “Substantial Gender Gaps Persist Across Many Countries,” states the intended conclusion before readers inspect the individual data points. Instead of using a neutral title such as “Distribution of Gender Gaps in Secondary-School Enrollment,” the wording frames the remaining large gaps as the main takeaway. The subtitle does provide important context about the 5-to-25-point filtering, which makes the framing somewhat more transparent, but the title still encourages readers to focus on persistence and severity rather than on the fact that many countries have relatively small gaps.

Final Reflection

When crafting these two opposing visualizations, it was interesting to discover how information from the same dataset could be interpreted so diversely and manipulated in such a way to explore separate narratives. Performing the straightforward task of simply analyzing the enrollment rates of males and females across each specific country and grouping them by values of 5 percentage points was effective. It was concrete and utilizing a stacked horizontal bar graph told the story very clearly that, from 2000 to 2018, an additional 10% of countries worldwide decreased the enrollment rate difference between men and women to below 5 percentage points. With our second visualization, it required a lot more thought. We needed to convince the audience that the decrease in enrollment rate differences wasn’t as significant as we previously highlighted. To complete that, we utilized a data cutoff and distinct color focuses. By excluding the data of countries with enrollment rate gaps between males and females of less than 5 percentage points, we made the more immensely gapped countries seem more plentiful. I was also surprised by how well singularly analyzing a time period worked. Instead of comparing separate years like the first visualization, by only observing one and not highlighting the previous trend, we convince the viewer that the gap and differences have always been true. That level of cunning was unique. Our use of color for the second visualization also made the 10 and 15 percentage point gap countries appear more distinct than the 5-10 percentage point gap ones. This was done using a gradient from bright yellow to a bold eye-catching red, exemplifying just how many countries had a larger gap in enrollment rates.

Observing how different context and techniques can be utilized to portray a message, it begs the question how would one define what a true “ethical analysis and visualization” is? Through this process, we started to develop our own understanding, as it didn’t simply mean portraying the raw data with accuracy, but also doing so in ways that didn’t intentionally withhold information from the viewer. An ethical visualization should be developed with design choices that don't exaggerate or obscure details in the data set to force a polarizing narrative. The goal in creating an ethical visualization would be to utilize simple attention grabbing techniques to casually guide the viewers’ attention to a genuine trend without cropping out the dataset’s formal structure. With our newly developed definition of “ethical analysis and visualization” in mind, we must consider if the actions we are taking to guide the audience’s eye to a pattern are genuine or misleading. The boundary between “acceptable” persuasive choices and the “misleading” ones should depend on whether the visualization hides important counterevidence or exaggerates the prevalence or severity of a pattern. It would be acceptable if a choice emphasizes part of a real pattern while still giving readers enough context to interpret it accurately. When a visualization uses cutoff or only the part of data that is beneficial to its illustration, then this visual choice is misleading, as it hides the overall correct pattern and displays incomplete data to readers.
